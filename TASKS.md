## Netcode audit — 2026-10-13

Audit requested by the human on 2026-10-13: what in the network code can cause freezes, lag, stutter or rubber-banding for a European user (about 200 ms of ping to Agni, simulators that lag now and then), and where Aurora can do better than Firestorm on the client side. Read: `crates/aurora-net` (circuit, session, capabilities, event queue, fetcher, object cache), the viewer side that drives it (`agent.rs`, `world/`, `scene/textures.rs`, `scene/meshes.rs`, `app.rs`, `ui/perf.rs`) and the matching Firestorm sources (`indra/llmessage`, `indra/llcorehttp`, `indra/newview`). Nothing here was measured on the grid by the audit: every figure marked "computed" is arithmetic from constants read in the code, and each item is one PR with its before / after measurement. The messages, capabilities and what the simulator expects do not change; only the client side does. A visible difference from Firestorm is said so in its item and is the human's decision.

> Today's grid logs (three sessions of 2026-10-13, RTX 4090, 500 to 1,500 fps): the `events` step costs 0.54 ms per frame at most on a one-second average, and the worst frames (0.4 to 1.5 s) are the first frame and waits outside the processor (`between`), not the network. But the logs hold no line about the network itself: no ping, loss, resend, queue age or input delay. Hence item 1 before anything else.

> Already better than Firestorm, to keep as it is: the network runs on its own runtime and is not tied to the frame rate (Firestorm reads the socket, flushes acks, resends and pings once per frame, with 20 ms or 400 messages per frame: `LLAppViewer::idleNetwork`, llappviewer.cpp); the UDP circuit and the seed capability start together at login (Firestorm waits for the capabilities and a decode loop before UseCircuitCode: llstartup.cpp STATE_SEED_CAP_GRANTED); cache misses are requested as each ObjectUpdateCached is read (Firestorm: once per frame); `Far` is sent in full on arrival (Firestorm: half the draw distance, then +10% per second: llviewermessage.cpp `send_agent_update`); downloads go through one priority queue with time-boxed results on the main thread (llcorehttp is first in, first out and its texture results are picked up by a sweep that takes 20 frames or more to go round: `LLViewerTextureList::updateImagesFetchTextures`); a disk cache hit never touches the network; our avatar's extrapolation tapers from 2 s to 3 s on a silent circuit (Firestorm hands `InterpolationTime` and `InterpolationPhaseOut` to the wrong setters, llviewerobjectlist.cpp `setPhaseOutUpdateInterpolationTime( interp_time )`: full speed for 3 s, then a dead stop).

Suggested order: 1 (measure), 2, 5, 7, 8, 13, 14, 15, then the rest; 3 and 4 are the ones that change what the user feels most and need the human's decision.

**Input latency and our own avatar**

- ⬜ **2. Control changes sent at once (alignment with Firestorm).** A key press or release is written to a shared structure that the session reads on a 25 ms tick (`session.rs` `run_session`), and a change is only sent when 100 ms have passed since the last AgentUpdate (`AGENT_UPDATE_INTERVAL`, `maybe_send_agent_update`). While walking, turning or moving the camera the updates already flow at 10 per second, so a press or a release waits up to 125 ms (about 60 ms on average, computed), on top of the round trip; a press and its release inside one window are never sent at all, and a release sent late makes the avatar overshoot. Firestorm sends any change of control flags in the same frame, then one follow-up update, limits the other changes to 125 per second and keeps 1 per second alive (`send_agent_update`, llviewermessage.cpp: `MAX_AGENT_UPDATES_PER_SECOND`, `control_flags_follow_up`); its thresholds are also finer than ours (camera 0.01 m against 0.1 m in `AgentControls::significantly_differs`). To do: the main thread hands each control change to the session (which wakes up for it instead of waiting for the tick), flags sent at once with the follow-up, the other fields with Firestorm's thresholds and its 125 per second. Unit tests of the send rule; on Agni: the delay of item 1 before and after, short taps (nudge, jump), stop distance when releasing the key while running.
- ⬜ **3. Movement prediction of our own avatar (to be decided by the human: visible difference from Firestorm).** Like Firestorm, Aurora moves our avatar only when the simulator's update comes back (`agent.rs`: "keyboard input only controls orientation and the flags"; PR #9 removed the key-driven physics on the grid to match `LLViewerObject::processUpdateMessage`): at 200 ms of ping the avatar starts and stops a fifth of a second after the key, more when the simulator lags. A modern client predicts: on a key press the drawn avatar starts moving at the speed the simulator will give it, the inputs are kept with their send time, and each server update is compared with what was predicted for that moment; the remaining error is blended away instead of being shown as a jump. Limits to state plainly: the simulator's physics cannot be reproduced exactly (prim collisions, slopes, pushes, scripted movement, vehicles are unknown to the viewer), so the lead must stay bounded (about one round trip of movement: 0.6 m walking, 1 m running at 200 ms, computed), fall back to the server alone when the error repeats (wall, script), and never apply when seated or under server autopilot. **Other avatars and moving objects**

- ⬜ **5. **UDP circuit when the link or the simulator lags**

- ⬜ **7. Receive and send buffers of the UDP socket.** The socket is bound with the system's sizes (`UdpSocket::bind` in `run_session`; 64 KiB by default on Windows, to confirm with the size logged); Firestorm asks for 800,000 bytes in reception and 200,000 in emission (`RECEIVE_BUFFER_SIZE`, `SEND_BUFFER_SIZE`, net.cpp). On arrival the main region and up to eight neighbours send their objects at once: any pause of the session loop (item 8) then overflows the buffer, and what is lost is either resent a second later or, for the unreliable position updates, gone (jump at the next one). To do: the same sizes as Firestorm, the size obtained logged; the counter of sequence gaps of item 1 shows the effect.
- ⬜ **8. No file work on the session loop.** The object cache of a region is read when its RegionHandshake arrives (`on_region_handshake` → `RegionObjectCache::load`) and every dirty cache is rebuilt in memory and written every 120 s, on `DisableSimulator` and at the end (`Session::tick`, `remove_sim`, `RegionObjectCache::save`: up to 60,000 entries per region), all on the task that reads the socket. During that time nothing is received, acknowledged or answered: the simulator's ping measure grows, its resends start, our AgentUpdates wait. Firestorm also reads the cache on its main thread (llviewerregion.cpp, before RegionHandshakeReply). To do: reads and writes on a blocking task, the reply to the handshake sent when the cache is loaded (its "cache empty" bit needs it), the write from a snapshot; the longest pause of the loop in the line of item 1.
- ⬜ **9. Seed capability: a failure must not be final.** `resolve_seed` tries 4 times in about 5 s, then the viewer shows "capabilities unavailable" and never asks again (`caps_requested` stays set in `request_caps` / `on_caps`): on a simulator that lags at the wrong moment the region stays without textures, inventory or teleports until a relog. Firestorm tries 30 times (`MAX_CAP_REQUEST_ATTEMPTS`, llviewerregion.cpp). Capability requests also have no overall timeout (`caps_http` only has a connection timeout): one that hangs never returns. To do: retries up to Firestorm's count with a capped delay and some jitter, a timeout on every capability request, the alert only when everything failed, and a new attempt on the next region change.
- ⬜ **10. Event queue under errors.** Statuses 499 to 504 are polled again after 100 ms whatever the time the request took and without counting as failures (`event_queue_loop`, `caps.rs`): a simulator that answers an error at once receives 10 requests per second for as long as it lasts. Firestorm treats these statuses as "no event" only after 10 s; sooner they are errors, with a wait of 1 s + 3 s per error and the session closed after 15 on the agent's region (lleventpoll.cpp `MIN_SECONDS_PASSED`, `EVENT_POLL_ERROR_RETRY_SECONDS`). To do: the same rule, with jitter; keep the immediate poll after a normal timeout. **To take first — found in the logs on 2026-10-13:** the failure counter of the loop is only reset by a reply that carries events, and a long poll that ends without one is cut as a transport error about once a minute ("event queue error: error sending request", present in every grid log, 2 in the 92 s of the reference of item 1). In a place where nothing happens the errors add up, and at the 21st the loop gives up for good ("event queue for … giving up", seen in the log of a long session of 2026-10-09 after twenty-odd minutes of errors a minute apart): from then on no TeleportFinish, no chat session, no parcel event reaches the viewer until a relog. The rule above fixes it: a poll that lasted more than 10 s is "no event", not an error, whatever ended it; errors only count when they come fast and in a row.
- ⬜ **11. Bandwidth asked from the simulator (alignment with Firestorm).** `send_complete_agent_movement` sends fixed throttles: 1,788 kb/s in total, of which 800 for the objects (Task). Firestorm's default (`ThrottleBandwidthKBPS` 3000 × 1.5 at start) asks for 4,500 kb/s, about 1,530 for the objects (computed from `BW_PRESET_500` / `BW_PRESET_1000`, llviewerthrottle.cpp), and lowers it by steps of 10% when the loss exceeds 3%, every 5 s. To do: the same setting, split and adjustment (Preferences › Network, Firestorm's default). To measure on Agni by the human with item 1: the object rate on arrival before / after — whether the simulator honours more than it does today is not known from the client sources.
- ⬜ **12. Resends, later.** The resend delay is 3 × the smoothed ping, between 0.5 and 3 s, 4 resends (`Circuit::tick`); Firestorm: 5 × its average, 1 s at least, 3 resends (message.h). Ours is already shorter at 200 ms (0.6 s against 1 s), but the ping is only measured every 5 s and starts at 300 ms. To do once item 1 gives numbers: round trip also measured on the acknowledgement of reliable packets that were not resent, delay from the average and its variation, resends in sequence order (today in the order of a hash map), and a send refused by the system retried at the next tick instead of waiting for the resend delay (`raw_send` only counts it).

**Loading: assets**

- ⬜ **13. HTTP/2 for the assets, and concurrency that adapts.** `reqwest` is built without its `http2` feature (`crates/aurora-net/Cargo.toml`): every asset goes over HTTP/1.1, one request per connection, 24 connections for all kinds (`Fetcher::new` in `lib.rs`). At 200 ms per round trip that is a ceiling of about 120 requests per second (computed), for regions that need thousands of textures at two requests each. Firestorm is not better: HTTP/1.1 pipelining 5 deep on 8 connections for textures, HTTP/2 only behind a test variable (`_httpoprequest.cpp`: `getenv("VIEWERASSET")`). To do: enable HTTP/2, log the protocol each asset host negotiates (first to do on Agni by the human: an agent does not connect), and when it is HTTP/2 raise the requests in flight well above 24 on one or two connections, bounded by the decode queue; in both cases lower the concurrency on 503 or timeouts and raise it again when all is well, instead of a fixed number. Measure: time until every texture of a region is loaded, cold cache, before / after.
- ⬜ **14. Requests that follow what is wanted: cancel and re-prioritise.** Nothing in the viewer ever calls `Fetcher::cancel`, and a request keeps the priority it had when it was queued (`scene/textures.rs`: `need_px` is still 0 at that moment, so every new texture enters at the same rank, first come first served). After a camera turn or a teleport, thousands of requests for what is no longer wanted keep their place in front of what is on screen; the queue of the region left still downloads after arrival. To do: a fetcher message that changes the priority of a request still queued (sending the request again today starts a second download once the first is in flight); cancel when a texture or a mesh has no user left, the body of a download in flight dropped when it is large; on a teleport, the queue of the region left emptied except what our avatar wears (Firestorm clears every texture request at teleport: `LLViewerTextureList::updateImages`, and never cancels a request in flight either).
- ⬜ **15. One round trip to the first pixels.** The first request of a texture is a fixed 16 KiB (`INITIAL_BYTES`); when that is not enough for the wanted level, the data is not decoded and a second range is asked (`TextureStreamer::update`, "fetch more"): two round trips, 400 ms and the queue, before anything but grey for whatever is larger than about 128 pixels on screen. Firestorm does the same in two steps (600 bytes, then the range: `FIRST_PACKET_SIZE`). To do: decode what the first reply allows while the second range is in flight (the code that picks the level exists just above), and size the first range from the size on screen when it is known, so that most textures need a single request — which also halves the number of requests against the ceiling of item 13.
- ⬜ **16. Priorities between kinds of assets.** Every mesh is queued at 1e12, settings and landmarks at 1e12, materials and sounds at 9e11, animations at 8e11, textures at 1e5 at most plus their size on screen (`scene/meshes.rs`, `scene/anim.rs`, `scene/sounds.rs`, `scene/settings.rs`): on arrival in a region full of meshes the animations of the avatars, our own bakes and the sky wait for the whole mesh queue, far meshes included. Firestorm gives each kind its own connections (12 for the assets, 8 for textures, 8 for meshes: `init_data` in llappcorehttp.cpp), so an animation never waits behind meshes. To do: a band for what is small and urgent (our avatar's bakes and attachments, animations, nearby sounds, settings, interface images) with a few reserved slots, then meshes and textures ordered together by size on screen.
- ⬜ **17. Meshes: only what is drawn, and nothing heavy on the main thread.** A mesh is downloaded whole, every level of detail and its physics, in one request (`range: None`, `scene/meshes.rs`); Firestorm asks for the 4,096 bytes of header, then the range of the level it draws (`MESH_HEADER_SIZE`, llmeshrepository.cpp). One round trip instead of two is right for what is near; for far or small meshes it downloads megabytes that will not be drawn and holds a slot. And when a mesh arrives (network or disk cache), the main thread copies the whole body, parses the header and inflates the skin (`MeshStreamer::on_fetch`, `accept_data`): a long frame for a large mesh, since at least one result is processed per frame. To do: the header and the skin parsed in the job that read or received the data, the body moved instead of copied; a first range (header and small blocks) then the wanted level for meshes that are small on screen, the whole asset for the others; measure requests, bytes and the worst frame on an arrival.
- ⬜ **18. Failures that end for good, and one that never ends.** A mesh, an animation or a sound that failed 4 times is abandoned for the session (`scene/meshes.rs`, `scene/anim.rs`, `scene/sounds.rs`): an outage of the asset servers leaves invisible meshes and missing animations until a relog (Firestorm also gives up on a mesh level after its HTTP retries). A texture that does not decode is dropped and downloaded again about every 6 s, forever, because the download resets the failure counter (`TextureStreamer::on_fetch`). To do: a delay that grows to a ceiling without a final state for anything but a 404, reset on a region change; a separate counter of decode failures with a long retry.
- ⬜ **19. Decoding and sending to the GPU in the order of what is seen.** Decode slots are given in the order of the work list, not by priority, and nothing slows the decodes when the uploads pile up: a full staging pool falls back to writes on the main thread (`scene/textures.rs`, `PendingUploads`). To do: decode candidates picked like the uploads (`UploadPriority`: placeholder first, then size on screen), and no new decode while the bytes waiting for upload exceed a share of the pool.
- ⬜ **20. Small ones, to take with the items above.** Each downloaded body is copied once more when it is handed over (`body.to_vec()` in `fetch.rs`); the raw bytes of every mesh stay in memory for the session (`MeshStreamer`); the `sound/` and `settings/` cache folders are not in the list the cache trims (`cache.rs` `DIRS`).

**Loading: arrival in a region, and frame times**

- ⬜ **21. A region that comes back in reach is applied in one frame.** Returning to a region left less than a minute ago puts back all its objects and replays every event received meanwhile inside a single call (`World::unpark_region`, `world/parked.rs`), outside the time box of the events; parking does the same in the other direction. To do: both spread over frames like the rest of the arrivals (the nearest objects first); measure the worst frame on a teleport there and back.
- ⬜ **22. Network events: two lanes and a budget tied to the frame.** The main thread applies the events of the network in arrival order for 8 ms per frame at most (`App::frame`, "network events (time-boxed)"). On this PC the box is far from full; on a slower one or in a dense arrival, 8 ms is more than a whole frame at 165 Hz, and the position of our avatar, the chat and the teleport messages wait behind thousands of object updates. To do, after the queue age of item 1 shows it happens: what is small and urgent (updates of avatars, removals, teleport, chat) ahead of the bulk (full updates, cache hits) while keeping the order of the messages of one object, and a budget that is a share of the frame time. Firestorm: 20 ms or 400 messages per frame for everything, in order.
- ⬜ **23. Performance window: a graph that shows hitches rather than noise.** The graph draws the raw rate of each of the last 240 frames (`ui/perf.rs` `graph`): at several hundred frames per second a variation of half a millisecond moves the line by hundreds of images per second, so it saws even when nothing is felt. To do: a line smoothed over a short window with the worst frame of each window drawn apart (what the eye feels), the choice of frame time in milliseconds, and the marks of item 1 (corrections, pauses of the network loop) on the same time axis. The true periodic spike already listed in "Optimisation" (`recompute_wants`, one frame in thirty) stays there.

    - ✅ what avatars wear is placed by the GPU (`scene/anchors.rs`): each bone palette ends with anchors, rigid world matrices written every frame — the first places the skeleton, the following ones the attachment points of the body as they are posed (47 points, 208 matrices per skeleton instead of 192). ## Performance audit — 2026-10-13

What limits the frame rate, the smoothness, the memory and the loading time in a place full of avatars, **the most rewarding first**. Reading taken by the human on Agni on 2026-10-13: release build of `main` (`09830247`), login at Knotty beach, 84 s online (about 18,700 frames) with their own settings — 2560 × 1369, SMAA, shadows "High", SSAO, SSR, 16 full avatars, draw distance 96 m, no fps cap, no vertical sync (`Mailbox`) — on a GeForce RTX 4090 and a Core i9-14900K (8 performance and 16 efficiency cores, 32 logical), 32 GB. `AURORA_PROFILE=1` and, from outside the viewer every 1.5 s, the process memory, the CPU time of each named thread, the GPU load and the video memory per process, and `nvidia-smi`; no other viewer on the GPU during the reading.
